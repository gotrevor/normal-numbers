Digits, arithmetic, and what we can actually prove

How Irregular Is a Number?

Irrational, transcendental and normal are three different questions about the same real number, asked with three different instruments. Only the first two are usually answerable.

Three claims, two subjects

The two lines you quoted are a status ledger for one constant: someone has listed what is known about it, and graded the cost of each fact.

arithmetic

Irrational

Not equal to any p/q. Equivalently, in every base its expansion fails to be eventually periodic.

trivial = it falls out of the construction. For a number built by concatenating digits you can see the aperiodicity; no theorem is invoked.

arithmetic

Transcendental

Not a root of any nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients. Strictly stronger than irrational: √2 is irrational but algebraic.

known = there is a citation, not an observation. For Champernowne's constant it is Mahler 1937, and it rides on deep Diophantine approximation.

statistical

Normal

In base b, every block of k digits occurs with limiting frequency b−k. Absolutely normal means this holds in every base b ≥ 2.

A different subject entirely: not how the number relates to ℚ, but how its expansion is distributed. Almost always open.

The first two are claims about a number's relationship to the rationals. Normality is a claim about the statistics of one expansion, and it is base-relative in a way the others are not. That is why they sit on different axes rather than on one ladder.

The picture

Why the third one is hopeless

Irrationality and transcendence each rule out one algebraic relation. Normality asserts an infinite family of statistical constraints, separately in every base, and there is no known mechanism that turns a closed-form definition into control over digit frequencies. So the ledger you are reading is the normal shape of such a ledger: the arithmetic entries are settled, the digit entry is open or was engineered in by construction.

Two facts make the gap concrete. Borel proved in 1909 that almost every real is absolutely normal, so the property is generic in measure. But the non-normal numbers, despite forming a null set, have full Hausdorff dimension and are topologically generic. Typicality answers differently depending on which notion of typical you pick, which is a warning about the whole enterprise of grading irregularity with one number.

Two axes, and they disagree

Once you ask how irregular rather than whether, the question splits. One axis measures how well rationals sneak up on the number. The other measures how random its expansion looks. A number can be extreme on either and ordinary on the other.

The axes genuinely disagree about what irregular means. Liouville numbers are maximally extreme in approximation, and that extremity comes from being highly structured: a sparse, fast-converging series with almost all digits zero. Meanwhile the golden ratio is the single worst-approximable number in existence and is conjectured to have perfectly random-looking digits. Pick your instrument before you rank anything.

A third expansion, and a fourth

Base-b normality is not the only digit story a real number has. The continued fraction is a second expansion with its own notion of typical: partial quotients should follow the Gauss-Kuzmin law, their geometric mean should tend to Khinchin's constant, and the denominators should grow at Lévy's rate. Call that CF-normal.

Neither notion is known to imply the other. Producing a single number that is absolutely normal and CF-normal was open until Becher and Yuhjtman did it in 2019, with an algorithm rather than a measure-theoretic existence argument. That gap is the honest measure of how loosely these instruments are tied together: two reasonable definitions of a random-looking real, and it took a construction to show they can hold at once.

Where the diagram is, on the web

There is no standard one. The familiar Venn diagrams stop at algebraic and transcendental, because those nest cleanly and normality does not: it cuts across the algebraic boundary, it is base-relative, and its most interesting region is empty of examples while being conjecturally full. Diagrams that would be honest about that are rare, which is why this page draws its own.
