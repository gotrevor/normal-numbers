Digits, arithmetic, and what we can actually prove

How Irregular Is a Number?

Irrational, transcendental and normal are three different questions about the same real number, asked with three different instruments. A fourth, whether any algorithm can print its digits, sits underneath all three. Only the first two are usually answerable.

Claude (Claude Code) wrote this page, working under my direction. I would rather say that plainly than have you wonder: the sources are linked, and the formalization claims name the repository and revision they were checked against. Trevor Morris

Four claims, three subjects

This page started from two lines of a status ledger for a single constant, irrational: trivial; transcendental: known;, which grade not the strength of each property but the cost of establishing it. Those grades are the way in.

arithmetic

Irrational

Not equal to any p/q. Equivalently, in every base its expansion fails to be eventually periodic.

trivial = it falls out of the construction. For a number built by concatenating digits you can see the aperiodicity; no theorem is invoked.

arithmetic

Transcendental

Not a root of any nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients. Strictly stronger than irrational: √2 is irrational but algebraic.

known = there is a citation, not an observation. For Champernowne's constant it is Mahler 1937, and it rides on deep Diophantine approximation.

statistical

Normal

In base b, every block of k digits occurs with limiting frequency b−k. Absolutely normal means this holds in every base b ≥ 2.

A different subject entirely: not how the number relates to ℚ, but how its expansion is distributed. Almost always open.

computability

Computable

Some algorithm, handed n, prints the nth digit. Turing's 1936 definition, and the one that made “real number” an object a machine can hold.

free, and that is the catch. Naming a number nearly always means describing a procedure, so the answer is yes for every constant on this page except Ω, and no for almost every real.

The first two are claims about a number's relationship to the rationals. Normality is a claim about the statistics of one expansion, and it is base-relative in a way the others are not. That is why they sit on different axes rather than on one ladder. Computability is a claim about neither: it asks what it costs to produce the number at all. It sits underneath both axes rather than on either, and it is the only one of the four whose answer is almost always yes.

The picture

Why the third one is hopeless

Irrationality and transcendence each rule out one algebraic relation. Normality asserts an infinite family of statistical constraints, separately in every base, and there is no known mechanism that turns a closed-form definition into control over digit frequencies. So a ledger like the one above has the shape it does for a reason: the arithmetic entries are settled, the digit entry is open or was engineered in by construction.

Two facts make the gap concrete. Borel proved in 1909 that almost every real is absolutely normal, so the property is generic in measure. But the non-normal numbers, despite forming a null set, have full Hausdorff dimension and are topologically generic. Typicality answers differently depending on which notion of typical you pick, which is a warning about the whole enterprise of grading irregularity with one number.

Computability the axis underneath: can anything print it?

There is a fourth question, and it is easy to miss precisely because the answer is yes for every number in the diagram above. Is the number computable: is there an algorithm that, handed n, returns the nth digit? Turing introduced the reals this way in 1936, and the answer sorts the line in a way neither of the other two axes does.

So yes: uncomputable implies transcendental, hence irrational. Every rational is computable, and so is every algebraic number, since you can isolate the roots of its polynomial to any precision you like. Anything uncomputable is therefore outside both. That single edge is the whole of the traffic between this axis and the arithmetic one, and it runs one way: π, e and every other transcendental with a name are computable, so the converse fails about as widely as a converse can.

The trap: there is no such thing as an uncomputable integer. Uncomputability is a property of infinite objects, and every finite object is printed by the program that just prints it. So the axis says nothing about 7, and nothing about BB(748) either. The busy beaver function is uncomputable (Radó 1962); each of its values is one particular integer, and one particular integer is computable, trivially.

What is true is stranger, and it is what the phrase “uncomputable integer” is usually reaching for. Stefan O'Rear built a 748-state Turing machine that halts exactly when ZFC is inconsistent, sharpening Yedidia and Aaronson's 7910-state machine. If ZFC is consistent it cannot prove that machine runs forever, so it cannot pin down BB(748). A program that prints that integer exists, and no theory we are willing to trust can tell us which program it is. Computable quantifies over algorithms that exist, not over algorithms we can find.

Now count. There are countably many programs, so there are countably many computable reals, and all three nested regions above are a measure-zero speck inside a line of measure 1. Every theorem on this page of the form almost every real is … is therefore silent about every number that has ever been named. Borel's 1909 result says absolute normality is generic; it says nothing whatsoever about π, and that is not a weakness of the proof, it is the shape of the situation.

Which is why making these proofs effective is a real part of the subject's history rather than a footnote. Sierpiński constructed an absolutely normal number in 1917 and Turing wrote an algorithm for one around 1937, unpublished until 1992 and with a gap; a genuinely computable example waited for Becher and Figueira in 2002, and a polynomial-time one for Becher, Heiber and Slaman in 2013, at just above quadratic time per digit. The Becher–Yuhjtman witness this page keeps pointing at is of exactly that kind. Its content is not that such a number exists, which measure theory hands you for nothing, but that a program prints it.

And there is a ceiling, which is the fact that earns this axis its section. No computable real is random in any effective sense at all, not even the weakest one on the lattice below: if x is computable then {x} is a null class an algorithm can close in on, so a computable number is never even Kurtz random. Absolute normality is the top of what a computable number can reach. That is the same fact as the two dimension rows in the instrument table disagreeing about Champernowne's constant, which has finite-state dimension 1, the maximum, and effective Hausdorff dimension 0, the minimum, because K(x↾n) = O(log n) for anything a program prints. Finite automata see a perfectly random string. A Turing machine sees a short program.

Two axes, and they disagree

Once you ask how irregular rather than whether, the question splits. One axis measures how well rationals sneak up on the number. The other measures how random its expansion looks. A number can be extreme on either and ordinary on the other.

The axes genuinely disagree about what irregular means. Liouville numbers are maximally extreme in approximation, and that extremity comes from being highly structured: a sparse, fast-converging series with almost all digits zero. Meanwhile the golden ratio is the single worst-approximable number in existence and is conjectured to have perfectly random-looking digits. Pick your instrument before you rank anything.

The digit side, all the way up

Normality is the bottom of a long ladder, and the ladder has one idea in it: a sequence is random against an adversary if no gambler of that kind, betting on the next digit at fair odds, can make unbounded money. Change the class of gambler and you change the notion.

Normality is the case where the gambler has finite memory. That is Schnorr and Stimm's theorem, 1972: a sequence is normal in base b exactly when no finite-state gambler wins on it. It sits at the bottom because a finite automaton cannot even count, and it is still open for π because defeating every finite-state gambler is a statement about all block frequencies at once.

Two things fall out of that picture. Every effective notion from Schnorr randomness upward gives you absolute normality for free, so normality is what survives when you throw away all computability and keep only the statistics. And the converse fails as badly as it can: Champernowne's constant is normal and computable, so a computable gambler beats it trivially, which is why it is at the bottom of the left column and off the right column entirely.

The one caveat worth stating: Schnorr randomness implying absolute normality is the version I would re-derive before quoting it, and where exactly Kurtz randomness sits relative to normality is a question I have checked only to the level of a literature search, not a proof.

Status board

The diagrams above carry mathematics, and that is all they should carry: an arrow means an implication holds, a crossed link means it fails, a ? means nobody knows. Whether a given edge has been machine-checked is a different question with a different answer for every edge, and it belongs in its own object rather than as a second layer of glyphs on a drawing that is already dense.

So: one row per claim in the picture, with its mathematical status and its formalization status kept apart. The discipline is that every edge gets a row, including the one-line ones, because a missing row and a hard gap should not look the same.

A third expansion, and a fourth

Base-b normality is not the only digit story a real number has. The continued fraction is a second expansion with its own notion of typical: partial quotients should follow the Gauss-Kuzmin law, their geometric mean should tend to Khinchin's constant, and the denominators should grow at Lévy's rate. Call that CF-normal.

Neither notion is known to imply the other. Producing a single number that is absolutely normal and CF-normal was open until Becher and Yuhjtman did it in 2019, with an algorithm rather than a measure-theoretic existence argument. That gap is the honest measure of how loosely these instruments are tied together: two reasonable definitions of a random-looking real, and it took a construction to show they can hold at once.

Where the diagram is, on the web

There is no standard one. The familiar Venn diagrams stop at algebraic and transcendental, because those nest cleanly and normality does not: it cuts across the algebraic boundary, it is base-relative, and its most interesting region is empty of examples while being conjecturally full. Diagrams that would be honest about that are rare, which is why this page draws its own.
